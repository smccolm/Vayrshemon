## Unta correction requested during this project

In Coriolanus 5.6, the spoils speech uses the spoken Unta fraction for one third. The translation reads that fraction as the margin by which the spoils exceed the campaign costs. The wiki records the other possible reading, in which the spoils cover more than a third of those costs. Aufidius’s instruction at the funeral retains the written cardinal three and his promise to be one of the bearers.

Cymbeline’s opening keeps the approximation in “some twenty years” and “about some half hour.” Imogen’s intended prayer at the sixth morning hour takes an ordinal word. She places her farewell kiss between two words, which takes a cardinal. The six and the two belong to different constructions in the same speech.

Coriolanus’s alliance scene keeps written “hundred times” and “twelve times” as Unta frequency words, and twelve-to-seventy as an age range. “One half” of command is a single exact half-share, not one plus a half. The source’s half-dead metaphor also retains the Unta half-word, with its figurative scope explicitly recorded; it is not a measured fraction of life. A servant’s “worth six” is a sixfold value comparison. These contextual records distinguish exact numerical forms from whether a character is speaking literally, hypothetically or hyperbolically.

As You Like It keeps clock readings separate from durations: two hours and two o’clock both use Unta words, but their constructions differ. Oliver’s later report says one hour; that source discrepancy is retained. William’s five-and-twenty is exactly 25, and Touchstone’s hundred and fifty ways is exactly 150, each expressed once in native Unta words. A flight or “pair of stairs” is not silently converted into two staircases. The source-linked review records these distinctions alongside literal quantities and contextual nonnumeric uses.

**Author clarification, 2026-11-05: preserve the source's notation.** Source digits use Unta numeral glyphs; source number words use Unta words. Thus `20` uses the two Unta glyphs for base-twelve 18, while `twenty`/quantitative `score` uses ämēnō krizum. `3rd` uses the Unta 3 glyph plus the native ordinal ending; `third` remains venik’t. This supersedes the briefly considered glyphs-for-everything interpretation. Unta encompasses both written figures and spoken words. The chart's paired-triangle radix separator is the original font's colon slot, so written half uses `0:6` in legacy font encoding. Exact source values remain unchanged.

Quantity keys distinguish `numericNotation: words` and `numericNotation: digits`; validation rejects replacing source words by figures or figures by words. This applies to the active new editions. The already sealed historical Bibles are not rebuilt by the translation pipeline.

The Act 4 timing joke in *As You Like It* distinguishes cardinal one thousand from the exact fraction one thousandth, keeping both in words. Its local English reads “thousand part”; the source is not silently emended. Six thousand years stays exactly 6,000, expressed with Unta place values, and “forever and a day” retains its additional spoken one-day component. The native labels for Friday and Saturday use spoken ordinal six/seven plus day, explicitly within a Sunday-first **source-calendar naming convention**. The dictionary records that scope; the possible fasting-day allusion remains a review note rather than replacing the named days.

Numerical concepts must use Unta systematically. The original `Inputs/Origins_3_Unta_2.jpg` and `Inputs/Unta - Base 12.jpg` were visually checked. `tools/unta_language.py` records the digit names, the eleven irregular dozen-multiple names and the directly checked short place scales. For example, a score is decimal 20, base-twelve **18**, spoken **ämēnō krizum**; threescore is decimal 60, base-twelve **50**, **rivēum**. Decimal 100 is **änimus bārum**, not 144. Third is **venik’t**. Occurrence multiples use the cardinal with **venor**; magnitude multiples use the cardinal with **mem** (measure). These productive constructions are authored developments, not claims that every derived phrase appears on the original chart.

The chart's **choi** separator supports exact terminating fractions: a third is base-twelve **0.4**, **fōlemchoi bārum**; a half is **0.6**, **fōlemchoi ōdēum**. Nonterminating fractions use an explicitly documented exact parts-out-of-equal-parts construction, with no rounding. The uncertain highest chart exponent is not extrapolated. Ordinal order, amount, repetition, duration, ratio, fiscal score and physical folds need contextual distinctions.

Every authored source unit containing numerical candidates requires an explicit `numericReview`. Candidate detection is a review aid, not an automatic assertion that every “one,” “second,” “score” or “gross” is a numerical quantity. Numerical sense entries carry exact rational values and base-twelve forms. Act/scene numbers are read in Unta on the native side while the source English remains exact. Page numbering already uses Unta glyphs and remains protected from body text.

Human review can record additional quantitative senses in `semanticQuantityReview`, such as “moiety” meaning half. The source term must actually occur, and its Unta entry must preserve the exact value and the source's words-versus-figures choice. These decisions appear in the working wiki and the complete-work Forge review. This supplements the candidate scanner; it does not make every occurrence of an ambiguous term a number.

Inflected numerical language is reviewed too. Sonnet 115's “millioned accidents” uses the Unta word construction for a million with plural marking: rhetorical millions, not a claim of exactly one million events. The numerical unit stays exact while its contextual multiplicity remains indefinite. Candidate detection includes forms such as `millioned` without treating unrelated words such as `millionaire` as cardinal quantities. “Double penance” retains the Unta two-fold construction, and “first” keeps ordinal wording when the source spells it out.

Context controls numerical interpretation. Sonnet 120's “once” means formerly, whereas Sonnet 135 asks for one occasion and therefore uses Unta occurrence words. Sonnet 122's “score” records love rather than naming twenty. Sonnet 125's “seconds” uses an Unta second-grade material construction, not seconds of time or two objects. Sonnet 133 retains both factors in “thrice threefold” as Unta word constructions; the combined magnitude is ninefold, without replacing the authored expression by an unprinted nine. In Sonnet 136's explicit reckoning, numerical “none” is Unta zero in words, while the following existential nothing/something remains lexical. These contextual decisions, exact values and notation are recorded alongside each source unit.

Sound-dependent puns receive explicit loss notes. Sonnets 135–136 use the native desire root for volition and sexual metonymy, while the personal name Will is adapted as Wil. The name exception does not license borrowing English will as ordinary vocabulary. Sonnet 138 retains both physical lying together and falsehood through their distinct native roots, without claiming identical homophony. Source-linked notes also distinguish keyboard jacks from a personal name, a sea bay from a horse colour, a debt-security statute from a generic law, and exhaled breath from putrid smell.

Sonnet 152’s “twice” is twofold oath-breaking, paired with two and twenty oaths in Unta words.
