# Historical KJV 0.10-kjv.37 · Selves within quotations, fables and contested kingship

These additions and readings remain historical and excluded from modernization. Jonah remains frozen.

## One pronoun can change its referent inside a quotation

Judges7:2 puts divine plural-I around a quoted Israelite boast. The first I is the divine speaker who refuses to give victory to an oversized army. The inner my-own-hand saved me is Israel claiming autonomous collective agency: my-plural and plural-I now refer to the internally plural national self. The same morphology does not mean the two speakers are identical. Quotation boundaries, the named boaster and the predicates supply the change of referent.

Gideon's questions and fear use personal-I. The endangered people in6:13, excluded Ephraim in8:1 and the prospective polity seeking hereditary rule in8:22 use singular-we. Succoth's refusal in8:6 uses the city as one decision-making subject. When Gideon repeats it in8:15, its singular-we remains the quoted city's voice, not his own party. Personal possessives are not automatically collective because a man mentions his family.

## Dual speakers and distributed donors

Zebah and Zalmunna are explicitly two named speakers in8:21: their us is dual-we. No unprinted number is assigned to the spies, citizens or donors elsewhere. In8:25 the men promise to give and each casts his own spoil into the garment. This is plural-we of distributed individual contributors, following the singular-we political appeal. The distinction expresses how the group acts in that clause; it does not permanently assign a different pronoun class to the same humans.

Plural address to an army and singular address to Gideon remain separate in7:7: I-plural will save you-plural, but deliver into thy-singular hand. The three generations requested in8:22 are the named man, his son and grandson, not a Trinity reference. No divine person-count is inferred from an ordinary numeral or from the plural-I morphology.

## Angel, LORD and the voice after departure

The narrative labels the visitor angel of the LORD, then has the LORD look and speak, later calls him angel of God, and says the angel disappears before the LORD reassures Gideon. These changes remain in the translation. The covenant-maker's quoted I is plural-I; Gideon's own I remains personal. The translation does not add an identity equation, emissary theory or modern psychological diagnosis to resolve the sequence.

Gideon's accusation that the LORD has forsaken the people remains a real question and claim. His signs retain their conditional epistemology: he asks to know that the speaker speaks with him. The first fleece sign requires wet fleece and dry surrounding earth; the second reverses both conditions. The twice-repeated only-this-once plea remains twice, rather than being edited away because a second request sounds awkward.

## Divine causation and human claims remain attributed

The perfect delivery promise precedes the narrated victory. The enemy dreamer's personal-I reports his own dream; his fellow interprets the barley cake as Gideon's sword. Those are attributed speeches, not a new general rule for interpreting dreams. The LORD turns each man's sword against his fellow, and later God sends an evil spirit between Abimelech and Shechem. Neither agency is softened into an impersonal accident or changed into a clinical diagnosis.

The narrator also assigns blood responsibility to the killer and those who strengthened him. The evil spirit does not erase human participation. The final repayment and fulfilled curse remain the source narrator's explanation; the translation does not append an endorsement of collective punishment. Likewise, Gideon's refusal of rule remains beside his wealth, ephod, many wives and dynastic aftermath rather than becoming a tidied moral biography.

## Talking trees are selves without becoming literal history

Jotham's trees seek one ruler and speak as singular-we. Each olive, fig, vine and bramble answers as a personal-I within the fable. The olive's fatness honours God and man, and the vine's wine cheers God and man. Both provocative divine predicates remain literal in the translated fable; no substitution of priests, nonalcoholic drink or merely metaphorical pleasure is inserted.

The bramble offers its shadow and threatens fire against the cedars. Its inadequate protection and disproportionate destructive claim remain unglossed images. Jotham's suspended if-clause runs through the parenthesis about his father into the conditional rejoicing and reciprocal fire curse. Both directions of the curse retain their targets. The exclamatory verbal ending marks the optatives without using an unsupported exclamation-mark glyph.

## Civic rhetoric does not erase personal ambition

Abimelech's I am your bone and flesh is personal-I claiming kinship, not an automatic plural-self of every citizen. Shechem's our brother is the community's common kin claim. Gaal uses singular-we when asking why the city should serve Abimelech, then personal-I when wishing the people were under his hand. Zebul later repeats Gaal's singular-we inside a taunting quotation; it is not Zebul joining the rebellion.

The source says Gaal speaks to Abimelech without explaining how the message reaches him; the translation does not invent a meeting. Zebul's claim that visible people are mountain shadows remains his dismissive statement, not narrator confirmation. Liers in wait are ambushers, not people telling lies. The source's change from four companies to a later three-company deployment and two other companies is preserved event by event.

## Quantities and uncomfortable continuities

Twenty-two thousand depart and ten thousand remain; three hundred are selected, organized into three groups, with one named group of a hundred. About fifteen thousand remaining enemies and a hundred and twenty thousand fallen are distinct source quantities; their sum is not added as a new assertion. Seventy-seven officials and1700 gold shekels are exact, while other ornaments are explicitly excluded from the latter total. The tower deaths are about a thousand men and women.

Seventy sons, seventy brothers slain on one stone and Jotham's survival remain together. The translation does not repair seventy into sixty-nine or insert an extra genealogical count. Even-the-second-bullock in6:25 is retained without deciding that it necessarily means two different animals. Source Gilead, the other-side-of-Jordan phrase before the next crossing, the Ishmaelite label beside Midianites and the before-sunrise wording remain unreconciled.

## Sense distinctions that matter to the actual story

New lexical entries include shelter-den, encamp, broth, fleece, lap-drinking, brier, bramble, secure-at-ease, collar, skull and armourbearer. Lap-drinking differs from a body's lap; secure means unalert or at ease here, not fastened or invulnerable. Fleece is wool covering without an added assertion about skin. Brier and bramble retain distinct source labels with a shared thorn-bush form. Explicit names and referent distinctions account for the other32 entries; Ishmaelite inherits the earlier source variant's form with its own declared label.

Tear flesh uses rend, not the inherited eye-tear noun. Coming close to a door uses near, not close meaning shut. Light persons are reckless, not luminous. Kindness is not the kind meaning species. Remaining people are remainder, not resting people. Pitching camp never uses the resin noun. Gideon's offering is offered, not present-time; night-watch period and newly set watch activity remain distinguishable. These checks prevent superficially valid but semantically wrong dictionary lookups.
