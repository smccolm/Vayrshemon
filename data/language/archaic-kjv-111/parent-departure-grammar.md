# KJV historical register 0.10-kjv.110 — Psalms 25–36

This continues the exact sealed 0.10-kjv.109 head with 199 newly authored verses. Psalms reaches 511 verses through Psalm 36; the Bible remains incomplete. Previous translations, frozen Jonah and all prior seals remain unchanged. This archaic edition is permanently excluded from modernization and consolidation.

## Personal address and the requested plural divine self

The ordinary petitioner keeps personal `i`. Public testimony alone does not require plural-I: the speaker praising among a great congregation in 35:18 remains an individual. In 27:8 the quoted divine seek-my-face uses `mypl`, while the responding heart's human I and the plural addressed seekers retain their different roles. In 35:3 the petitioner asks for the reply `ipl thy/salvation be:pr`: plural-I speaks as one complex divine self. This is a requested quotation, not a report that a new revelation occurred. The personal-I of 31:5 is not retrospectively assigned to a later speaker who quotes it. The unlabelled teacher in 32:8 remains personal-I because the text does not explicitly identify the voice as a divine answer. None of these constructions adds a cardinal number of divine persons or a clinical interpretation.

## Worshipping whole and hostile chorus

The shared soul, help, shield and heart of 33:20–22 use `ourone` and `wesg`. In 34:3 a personal inviter enters a common act of praise: with-me becomes let-we-singular-exalt-together. In 36:9 singular-we shares the confession about seeing in the deity's light, followed by the personal protection plea of 36:11. Corporate singular is not inherently holy or favourable. The opponents of 35:21 claim `ourone/eye`, and their denied quotations in 35:25 use `wesg` for a united hostile boast. The construction column makes the difference in speaker visible. Plural addressees remain plural; no unprinted first-person pronoun is inserted into third-person descriptions just to increase the frequency of these forms.

## Conditional dependency and changes of tense

Psalm 27:13 uses explicit irrealis `would` with past-perfect and the unless clause. Past-perfect `pp` alone would have wrongly asserted an earlier collapse; it is not a generic counterfactual or participle marker. The as-though comparison of 35:14 retains its conditional dependency rather than asserting actual kinship. Psalm 32:5 distinguishes present acknowledgment, completed non-concealment, a quoted future confession and past forgiveness. Psalm 36:12 retains completed falling, present casting-down and future inability. Negative requests remain separate where the source separates them, including the four prohibitions of 27:9. Neither and nor can inherit the governing negative force; they do not become positive requests merely because the following verb is not given a second negative suffix.

## Historical source readings and unresolved references

Psalm 25:12 leaves its repeated he and chooser unresolved. Psalm 28:8 retains their-strength rather than importing a different witness. Psalm 32:9 preserves lest-they-come-near rather than reversing the supplied wording. Psalm 35:13 leaves the returned prayer in the speaker's bosom without explaining whether it succeeded or failed. Psalm 36:1 retains my-heart beside his-eyes instead of silently repairing the possessors. The unlabelled teaching voice of 32:8, the abrupt third-person this-poor-man of 34:6 and the shift from plural enemies to singular him in 35:8 remain visible. These are source-specific awkwardnesses, not gaps to fill with invented events or names.

## Four lexical additions and contextual sense control

The four new common entries are the horse's mouth-bit, contrite inward condition, slippery footing and the historical designation abject. They use declared inherited roots and introduce no new inflectional endings. A bridle-bit is not a small amount; slippery is unrelated to the inherited slip meaning a plant cutting. Contrite records crushed or humbled spirit without making a medical claim. Abject retains the source speaker's hostile social designation without endorsing it. The existing dictionary supplies the rest: covenant is the binding pledge, oracle is an inner sanctuary chamber in 28:2, and deserts are deserved recompense. Level paths differ from plain appearance; fashioning hearts uses shape rather than dress-fashion. Fast-standing uses firmness, while fasting in 35:13 uses food-abstention. Lightened faces use illumination, not burden reduction. Reins are kidneys, hosts are assembled armies, want is lack, and discover-forests is uncovering. The same source word can demand different entries; novelty is not manufactured where a suitable inherited word already exists.

## Personified organs, poetry and expressive force

The alien historical texture retains soul-lifting, instruction of kidneys, glory singing, bones speaking, the feminine soul's boast, a shared witnessing eye, protective wings, house-fatness and a river of pleasures. These are not flattened into modern abstract paraphrases. The contradictions of silence with roaring, integrity claims beside pleas for mercy, and death's dust being asked whether it can praise remain unresolved. Repetition such as give-to-LORD, voice-of-LORD and the paired wait imperatives is deliberate poetic structure. It must be distinguished from accidental mechanical repetition in newly composed language. Exclamatory morphology carries expressive addresses and repeated ah without requiring an unsupported Thimfora exclamation mark. Maxima remains determined by the original case pairs.

## Quantities and nonnumerical singulars

The new explicit quantities are one desired thing in 27:4, ten musical strings in 33:2 and not-one broken bone in 34:20. Their scopes differ: the final expression is a negative universal, not a claim that a body contains one bone. Generic one in 35:14 means a human mourner, not a newly counted person. Shared soul, heart and eye do not count the members of the congregation or chorus. Many waters, days, afflictions, enemies and all bones acquire no invented totals. The plural divine self remains a discourse construction rather than a numerical solution to later Trinitarian passages.
