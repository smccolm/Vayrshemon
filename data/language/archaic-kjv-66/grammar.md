## Singular-we as purpose, responsibility and testimony

The prophetic sons speak as one dwelling community(6:1–2), but each man contributes his own beam. Singular-we does not remove distributive individuals. The four leprosy men use plural-we for their separate lives and deaths(7:3–4); singular-we briefly presents their joint surrender plan. Their later shared failure to report, joint responsibility for silence, decision to tell, and unified report use singular-we(7:9–10). The inherited peace-holding expression uses possessive ourone for that shared silence. Collective perspective is chosen by discourse function, not by replacing every English we.

## Royal self and multiple levels of reported strategy

The Syrian king's camp possession(6:8) and capture instruction(6:13) express institutional royal self. Israel's king asks twice whether I-plural shall strike the captives(6:21), acting for the state. Personal I instead expresses his demand to know a suspected individual traitor(6:11), his inability to help the starving woman(6:27), and his self-curse(6:31). In7:12 royal plural-I introduces the king's theory about the siege. The city's threatened body is singular-we; inside his imagined enemy quotation, singular-we refers to the Syrian army. This is speculative nested speech, not narrator confirmation that an ambush existed.

## Actual pairs, anonymous people and quotation resets

The alarmed servant and Elisha are us-two in6:15–16; the unseen plural helpers remain distinct. The two women in the siege report use actual dual-we(6:28–29). The inner my-son in the earlier proposal belongs to the other woman; the reporting mother's my-son in the next verse refers to the child they boiled and ate. Human one denotes a worker, petitioner or unnamed servant. Cardinal one counts a tent, a time or a shekel. The plural council in6:11 permits the king to ask which individual of us serves the enemy; a singular-we would obscure that accusatory discrimination.

## Knowledge claims and unresolved speakers

Opening the servant's eyes and later opening the captured men's eyes are separate events with different experiencers. The supplied he in6:33 remains he; the translator does not confidently assign an ambiguous complaint. ## Unsparing siege narration and local temporal claims

The two mothers' reported cannibalism, the hidden second child, the king's threat to remove Elisha's head, and his inner sackcloth remain explicit. No invented moral or medical explanation softens those scenes. Syrian bands come no more in6:23, and afterward Benhadad besieges the city in6:24; both source claims remain without a fabricated permanent-peace chronology. ## Material language and two additions

Blindness is blind-state, the named visual condition, without a medical mechanism or loss of other senses. Cab names the volume unit in the siege price; no modern conversion is added. Borrowed equipment motivates the worker's cry. Dove dung remains dung, not a silently imported plant interpretation. Barnfloor uses the inherited threshing-floor term; grain scarcity and winepress scarcity are separate resource images.
