## Household plural-I does not replace bodily personal-I

Naomi's1:21 I went out full and returned empty is rendered with `ipl`, the plural-self of the bereaved household she voices. Her husband and both sons have died. This is an authored Vayrshemon interpretive choice: the woman speaks as the remnant of a once larger household. It is not a claim that the English I, or the source text behind it, bears plural morphology.

The surrounding distinctions constrain this choice. Her womb, age, possible husband and imagined sons in1:11–12 use personal-I. Her demand for the name Mara in1:20 is personal naming; her subsequent full/empty account voices the household. Ruth is physically beside her, so empty is not softened to mostly empty or turned into a literal census. The emotional claim can discount a living presence without the translator endorsing that discount.

## Two daughters-in-law, three travellers, then two

Orpah and Ruth answer together in1:10: dual-we will return with thee. Naomi is the addressee, not a third member of that we. Initially all three women walk toward Judah. Orpah kisses Naomi and departs; Ruth clings. Only after that change does the narrator explicitly say they two reached Bethlehem. The affection in Orpah's kiss remains without an added accusation of betrayal.

Naomi and Ruth later refer to Boaz as kin to dual-us, and Naomi says our kindred with the same two-person referent. The relationship includes kinship through marriage; dual does not assert shared biological parents. At the gate Boaz's our brother Elimelech addresses the other kinsman as the second member of the kin pair. Brother retains the source's range rather than being narrowed to a proved same-parent sibling relationship.

## Personal vows and several witnesses are different acts

Ruth's vow to follow, lodge, die and be buried uses personal-I. An individual commits herself to another woman's people and God; she is not already the voice of the whole adopted population. The source contrasts Orpah's gods with Ruth's God, and keeps the oath's death exception. No modern wedding formula replaces this allegiance to a mother-in-law.

Boaz's purchase declarations remain personal-I legal acts. The gate audience answers with plural-we witnesses: several people attest, rather than one undifferentiated corporate owner making the purchase. Rachel and Leah are explicitly two in the blessing's simile, but that comparison does not make the audience dual. Multiple referent sets can coexist in one sentence.

## Foreign affiliation remains after belonging is promised

Ruth continues to be called the Moabitess after her vow. The supervisor identifies the returning Moabite maiden; she calls herself a stranger and asks why she has been noticed. Boaz says he has been told of her actions, parents and birth land left behind. The translation keeps his second-hand knowledge distinct from personal witnessing.

Her deferential thy handmaid coexists with not being like one of his handmaidens. His daughter address is not a biological claim. These forms expose unequal standing and provisional welcome; they are not smoothed into a modern equal-status exchange. Mother's house in Naomi's dismissal remains mother's, rather than automatically substituting a father's household.

## Chance, deliberate provision and rest

The narrator says Ruth's hap brought her to Boaz's field portion. The translation keeps chance; it does not add a divine navigation claim. Boaz later deliberately orders handfuls left for her. Those two descriptions need not be collapsed. Access among sheaves, absence of reproach, the order that young men not touch her and permission to drink drawn water are concrete changes to her working conditions.

Rest can describe hoped-for security in a husband's house, a brief interval in the field house, Naomi's wish for Ruth's future, and Boaz's predicted inability to rest before the business ends. These uses are related but not substituted for one another. Source young men in Ruth's report remains beside maidens in the surrounding directions, without harmonizing their wording. Generic corn remains cereal grain rather than maize.

## Wings, garment edge and the limits of an explicit scene

Under divine wings in2:12 remains bodily imagery. In3:9 Ruth asks for the skirt of Boaz's garment to be spread over her; the new garment-edge entry preserves that concrete noun. The covering images can be read together without replacing the source's different words with one invented exact equivalent.

Feet remain feet. The narration of washing, clothing, lying at the grain heap, uncovering feet, midnight surprise and early departure contains no added sexual act. Merry does not establish drunken incapacity. Secrecy does not prove an unprinted event, and the translation does not claim to settle the interpretive debate. Naomi's odd who art thou, my daughter remains awkward instead of becoming how did it go.

## Nearer kin, conditional willingness and uncomfortable purchase

Boaz's willingness is contingent on the nearer kinsman. The source repeats the part of a kinsman; the translation keeps that repeated duty phrase. The first acceptance of redemption precedes fuller disclosure and refusal. The man fears damage to his own inheritance and transfers a legal right, using the inherited entitlement sense rather than the Judges right-conduct entry.

Advertise is inform. Redeeming and changing are redemption and exchange. The shoe transfer is testimony in the narrated former custom; no spitting or humiliation episode is imported from another law. Buy it also of Ruth and I purchased Ruth as wife remain the supplied legal language. The translator does not replace it with a modern consensual courtship description or pretend that every social implication has been resolved.

## More than one way to belong to a household

Ruth bears the child, Naomi takes him and becomes nurse, and the neighbours announce a son born to Naomi. Biological birth, caregiving and social household restoration remain different clauses. No second birth or invented lactation is added. The new nourish sense is feed-life for sustaining old age, distinct from the earlier infant-feeding sense.

The daughter-in-law who loves Naomi is said better than seven sons. Seven is a value comparison, not Naomi's former son count. The genealogy names Boaz as father of Obed beside the earlier legal goal of preserving the deceased man's name. The text is allowed to carry both relations without a translator's silent genealogical repair. Jesse is explicitly the father of David, avoiding attachment of both father labels to Obed in Vayrshemon's nested construction.
