# Historical KJV 0.10-kjv.72 — siege voices and deliverance

This continuation authors all 74 verses of 2 Kings 18–19. The 2 Kings working text now contains 565 verses. Whole-Bible coverage is 10,147 of 31,102 verses; 20,955 remain. This is an incomplete checkpoint, with the entire single-volume Bible still the deliverable. Earlier sealed editions and frozen Jonah remain unchanged and excluded from modernization.

## Royal plural-I, envoy personal-I and nested speech

Hezekiah's submission in 18:14 uses plural-I for the ruler accepting an assessment on his polity. In 18:20 the Assyrian message first quotes Hezekiah's claimed resources and then returns to the Assyrian speaker's against-me question. Both can use plural-self morphology without being the same person. Reporting clauses, source speaker names and the documented reading establish those boundaries; no new quotation particle is invented merely to make one passage easier. The royal promise to receive surrender and remove the population in 18:31–32, the mine-hand taunts in 18:34–35 and the dynastic my-fathers in 19:12 retain that representative perspective.

Rabshakeh's my-lord, I-will-deliver-horses, I-came-up and my-master-sent-me in 18:23–27 use personal I and possession: he is an envoy claiming an authorized role, not a new king or deity. His claim that the LORD ordered the attack remains a claim within his speech. It does not become the translator's certification of divine commission. The officials' courtesy I-pray-thee is similarly personal, even when several officials are named in the reporting clause.

## Singular-we community and ordinary plural officials

The quoted we-trust-our-God in 18:22 and the repeated us-deliverance claims in 18:30,32 use singular-we for the threatened community viewed as one body. Our-one is a possessive alias, never a free-standing substitute noun. The officials' we-understand-Syrian in 18:26 instead uses ordinary plural-we: several people possess linguistic competence. They are not a dual simply because they form one delegation, nor a singular collective because every first-person plural must sound unusual.

In 19:19 personal-I beseeches for singular-us before our-one God. The individual petition and collective beneficiary coexist within one clause. This is a deliberate grammatical tension, not a random mixture. The divine addressee remains singular thou and alone/only in the prayer. Those source words are not removed to make them agree superficially with the language's plural-self divine I.

## Divine plural self and the quoted invader

Isaiah reports the divine plural-I in 19:6–7 and 19:20. The prophet remains a messenger; plurality is not a count of prophets, angels or divine persons. In 19:23–24 the oracle quotes the Assyrian ruler's boast, so its plural-I and my-plural chariots/feet belong to the invader. At 19:25 the response returns to divine plural-I claiming ancient formation and present accomplishment. The same morphology supports distinct referents because identity follows discourse, not a fixed dictionary label.

The divine knowing, nose-hook/bridle threat and city-defense promise in 19:27–28,34 retain matching plural-self possession. David remains the servant, not a member added to the speaking self. Third-person LORD statements and the prayer's exclusive deity claim remain as supplied. Nothing here licenses a numbered Trinity; the later relevant passages still await translation.

## Offensive speech, concessions and bodily images

The full siege threat in 18:27 retains dung and urine. Urine receives an explicit substance entry instead of misusing the verb piss as an event. The pleasant land inventory in 18:32 remains attached to taking the people away: the invitation's propaganda is not transformed into a neutral agricultural promise. The prayer in 19:17 concedes the Assyrians' actual destruction before disputing the status of their victims' gods; the concession is not deleted.

The bruised reed pierces a leaning hand, the labouring body lacks strength for birth, Zion shakes a feminine head, the invader boasts of drying rivers with his feet, and the oracle threatens animal-like restraint through nose and lips. These remain bodily and uncomfortable. No medical explanation, sanitized metaphor or invented modern military mechanism replaces them. The source's they-arose/they-were-dead transition in 19:35 is retained without claiming that the dead resurrected to inspect themselves.

## Material senses and productive constructions

Six common entries distinguish conduit, fuller, urine, cistern, restraint-hook and physical lodging. Conduit is water-path; fuller is cloth-wash-person; cistern is water-store-pit; lodging is lodge-place. These are explicitly authored compounds, not claims of ancient attestation. The restraint-hook shares the established hooked-object form but records the coercive use separately from a suspension fastening.

Burden-bearing in 18:14 uses carry, while labour in 19:3 uses birth-bear and the surviving plant in 19:30 bears fruit. The established productive noun-to-verb pattern supports defence as the act of keeping attack away in 19:34. Physical clothing replaces a clothe-event nominalization. Strange waters in 19:24 means foreign waters, not inexplicably odd water. Laying cities waste uses making-desolate, not the rubbish noun. Springing crop uses grow, not the water-source spring. Bank in 19:32 is the existing siege-earthwork term. Corn remains cereal grain, host an army, recorder an official, and present a gift.
