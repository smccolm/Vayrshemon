# Historical KJV 134: vineyard selves, refused rest and fragile alliances

This continuation adds 108 authored verses, Isaiah 27–31. Isaiah reaches 605 verses; whole-Bible coverage reaches 18,308/31,102, with 12,794 remaining after validation. It inherits the corrected Proverbs copy from 132 unchanged. Earlier seals and frozen Jonah remain unchanged. The complete Bible is still unfinished.

## Divine plural-I and the rulers' singular-we
The explicitly named LORD in 27:3 speaks as plural-I, continuing through the fury/burning conditional in 27:4 and peace invitation in 27:5. The rulers' death-covenant boast in 28:15 uses singular-we and our-one; divine plural-I answers in 28:16–17. Ariel's siege in 29:2–3 and the mouth/heart accusation in 29:13–14 retain the divine plural-self. Possessive my-plural in 29:23 and 30:1–2 belongs to this divine speech. The secretive group in 29:15 and the demands for flattering prophecy in 30:10–11 use singular-we, as do the two horse-policy declarations in 30:16. There is no new divine-person count.

## Personal voices inside collective judgment
The prophet's hearing in 28:22 and teaching possessives in 28:23 remain personal. Both failed readers in 29:11–12 speak personal-I, but for different reasons: one faces a sealed book and the other is unlearned. The made object's I in 29:16 is personification, not divine speech. The unlabelled cry in 30:7 is read as the prophet's cry concerning Egypt; this is an explicit interpretive choice. The receiver of the LORD's words in 31:4 likewise remains personal-I. These distinctions preserve changes of speaker instead of globally replacing all first-person language.

## Source repetition remains a grammatical event
Precept upon precept and line upon line repeat in 28:10 and 28:13. The same formulation changes from teaching to an account of stumbling; repetition is necessary here. Both strange-work/strange-act pairs in 28:21, repeated Ariel in 29:1–2, doubled cries in 29:9, and repeated marvelous work in 29:14 remain. The night dreams in 29:8 retain hunger/eating/waking and thirst/drinking/waking separately. No variation generator replaces these with synonyms that erase the parallels.

## Bodily, material and agricultural senses
Eight common entries distinguish chalkstone, stammer, fitches, cummin, camel hump, beacon, winnowing fan and dry sieve. Fitches and cummin remain historical source labels without an asserted modern species. The winnowing fan is an implement, while inherited fan is a verb; the dry sieve is distinct from the inherited liquid-drainage container. Bunches of camels in 30:6 are bodily humps. Ear in 30:24 means plough; ear in 30:21 remains the hearing organ. Tables in 28:8 use meal furniture, while table in 30:8 is a writing tablet. The potsherd entry already denotes a pot fragment and can receive its two unsuccessful uses in 30:14. The menstrual-cloth image in 30:22 is retained, not replaced by vague dirt.

## Unresolved agency and difficult readings
Fury is not in me in 27:4 coexists with the conditional burning that follows. Divine care in 27:3 coexists with denied mercy in 27:11. The source's piercing serpent, red wine vineyard, nested he/him questions, horsemen in the threshing image, no haste in 28:16, as-Ariel repetition, grounded staff and battles of shaking remain as supplied. Lighting down in 30:30 is the arm's descent. The bridle causes error in 30:28; the deity brings evil in 31:2. Neither is silently changed to permission merely to avoid the source's philosophical difficulty.

## Numbers, physical direction and grammatical exclamation
The explicit quantities in 30:17 are one thousand, one and five. Sevenfold and seven days in 30:26 remain two separate sevens, not forty-nine. One by one in 27:12 is distributive each-by-each. Mighty and strong one in 28:2 and mighty One in 30:29 are substantivized expressions, not cardinal counts. Right things in 30:10 concern conduct/truth; right hand in 30:21 is spatial. Exclamatory morphology carries woe and vocative force where needed without adding an unavailable exclamation-mark glyph. Maxima generation remains unchanged.

## Named places and alien syntax
Four proper labels distinguish the local Perazim, Ariel-city, Hanes and Tophet. They inherit the historical naming convention without fabricated etymologies or an automatic merge with namesakes. The source's pronoun changes, collective singulars, nested relative clauses and mismatched images remain chunky. The translation does not turn those difficulties into contemporary explanatory prose; its notes hold the interpretive choices separately from the running text.
