## A plural-self can wield public force without becoming divine

Nahash's covenant demand in11:2 uses plural-I for a conqueror whose proposed bodily punishment will be carried out through his force. This is public embodied agency, not a new divine identity or a rule that every king must use a majestic plural. The demand's mutilation, condition and intended national reproach remain explicit. Jabesh answers as singular-we, a threatened town offering service and asking time to seek help.

Saul's14:37 inquiry about descending after the enemy likewise uses plural-I for the expedition acting through its king. It is distinguished from his personal-I in the excuse for offering sacrifice and his personal vengeance in14:24. The source supplies different roles; the language marks those roles without inventing additional speakers, multiple physical Sauls or counted divine persons.

## Personal testimony and public confession are different

Samuel's defense in12:1–5 remains personal-I: his age, gray head, childhood, sons, hands, alleged taking, oppression and restitution are his own. The answering witnesses use plural-we for multiple people who could have suffered individual fraud or oppression. This differs from singular-we in the remembered communal confession and the renewed plea not to die.

The king's anointed status and the LORD's witness are both present, while the final he is witness remains singular as supplied. Samuel's refusal to cease praying is also personal duty. A public speaker need not dissolve into a corporate self every time he addresses a crowd.

## The dual survives inside competing quoted plurals

Jonathan and the armour-bearer are a genuine pair. Their proposal, hiding-place alternative, ascent and sign interpretation use dual-we. The armour-bearer's answer uses personal-I to pledge his own participation. Jonathan's follow me locates his individual body ahead of his companion.

In14:9–12, imagined and actual Philistine speech uses plural-we for the garrison and dual-you for the visitors. The surrounding account returns to the pair's dual-we. Thus wait until we come to you does not accidentally turn the enemy group into Jonathan's pair. The pair's claimed victory expands to Israel's hand, but the number of climbers does not expand with the beneficiary.

## Consent, rescue and kingship retain coercion

Saul sends cut oxen with a threat that absentees' animals will receive the same treatment. The people's one consent follows this threat and fear of the LORD. The source's unanimity is retained without turning it into freely informed modern consent. Jabesh's apparent surrender message remains misleading in the setting of promised relief, rather than being rewritten as an open challenge.

After victory the people seek to kill Saul's earlier opponents; Saul refuses a death that day. This limited restraint does not deny the battle deaths just narrated. Renewing the kingdom at Gilgal remains a further public event beside private anointing and earlier selection. Later, the people again act collectively, this time rescuing Jonathan against the king's threatened execution.

## The history does not become tidy through translation

Samuel names Jerubbaal, Bedan, Jephthah and Samuel. Bedan is not silently replaced with Barak, and the speaker's own third-person name remains in the list. The Nahash explanation of requesting a king stays beside the earlier old-age/corrupt-sons explanation. Thunder and rain in wheat harvest indict the request even though the request has been granted.

The LORD's refusal to forsake the people for his great name remains beside the threat that people and king will be consumed if wickedness continues. Saul's one-year/two-year statement remains the supplied KJV wording without an inserted age or reconstructed chronology. A stated seven-day wait precedes Samuel's arrival just after the offering and his condemnation; no shorter wait is substituted to make blame straightforward. Jonathan strikes the garrison in13:3, while the report in13:4 credits Saul. Both agents remain where the source puts them.

## The oath produces physical and political consequences

Saul's food ban is framed by personal vengeance, until evening. Its later report says this day; the wording difference remains. Jonathan has not heard the oath. His honey and enlightened eyes retain the source bodily image, and his criticism still aims at a greater enemy slaughter, rather than becoming a wholly modern humanitarian speech.

Exhausted people fall on spoil and eat with blood. Saul's corrective instructions mention each ox and sheep, while the subsequent carrying report mentions each ox at night. The report is not expanded to match the instruction. His first built altar is retained despite the earlier offering; offering does not by itself establish that he built an altar then.

The proposed night pursuit would leave no man. Divine silence leads to Saul's sin inquiry, not an inserted narrator explanation of why no answer came. A first lot selects Saul and Jonathan; a second takes Jonathan. The short perfect-lot prayer stays the supplied wording without an expanded text from another witness. Jonathan's behold I must die retains its possible irony or protest without a definitive emotional diagnosis. The people's counter-oath and rescue prevent the announced death, and the Philistines eventually return to their own place. The no-survivor proposal is therefore not presented as fulfilled.
